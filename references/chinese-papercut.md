# Chinese folk paper-cut

Use `SCHEME_CHIN_PAPERCUT` as a physically plausible cut-paper system, not as red line art, lace, engraving, or a dense decorative filter.

## Visual model

- Treat the image as a folk narrative made from solid paper and open air. The positive paper mass and the negative cutouts must both describe the subject.
- Prefer immediately readable, lively silhouettes over anatomical accuracy. Figures, animals, plants, vehicles, and buildings may be gently exaggerated, but retain the source subject's identifying pose and structure.
- Keep the result visually flat: one paper plane, no modeled volume, bevels, cast shadows, stacked-paper depth, gradients, or painted highlights.
- Use a single saturated red paper color in final stamp artwork. Black-and-white historical examples are references for cut logic and value balance, not a request to change the scheme's red-paper identity.

## Composition systems

Choose the system that best fits the subject rather than forcing every image into the same ornamental frame:

- **Framed narrative:** a broad irregular border acts as the structural skeleton. People, animals, plants, ground lines, ropes, branches, or architecture connect naturally to the border or to one another.
- **Freestanding emblem:** one compact main silhouette stands on a strong ground strip, pedestal, branch, or other plausible support. Use this for a single person, animal, object, or concentrated action.
- **Mirrored or repeated scene:** use bilateral reflection or repeated panels only when rhythm, ritual, performance, or communal activity benefits from it. Allow small hand-cut differences; never create sterile digital symmetry.
- A border is optional. Add one only when it supports continuity, storytelling, or the stamp composition; do not use it as automatic decoration.

## Cut-paper logic

- Begin with one solid red paper sheet and remove clear negative shapes from it.
- Reduce the subject to a few broad connected masses with a recognizable silhouette.
- Keep load-bearing red bridges visibly wide and continuous. Prefer smooth scissor-cut curves, blunt knife-cut corners, and decisive openings.
- Build rhythm through linked gestures: touching hands, ropes, branches, reins, tails, stems, ground strips, or architecture can connect separate story elements without looking like arbitrary supports.
- Use one primary silhouette, one internal structural layer, and only a few symbolic accents chosen from the actual subject.
- Make enclosed holes intentional and spacious. Negative cuts should clarify a face, joint, limb overlap, animal marking, leaf vein, wheel, window, costume fold, or movement rhythm rather than merely add texture.
- Internal details must read as removed paper: use tapered slits, leaf-shaped openings, wedges, crescents, broad notches, and simple holes with enough width to survive cutting. Do not imitate pen lines inside a black or red mass.
- Vary curves and spacing by hand. Repetition may establish rhythm, but each repeated animal, leaf, feather, wave, or border notch should have slight differences in scale, angle, and spacing.
- If separate red pieces are necessary, make them clearly intentional components rather than accidental floating fragments.

## Translation from a photograph

- Identify the smallest set of anchors that makes the source recognizable: usually the outer silhouette, pose or directional action, one or two structural overlaps, and a few distinctive negative openings.
- Merge clutter into larger masses. Turn background detail into a plant, ground, cloud, architectural, or border connector only when it helps the story or physical continuity.
- Preserve the subject and action, but reorganize depth into a readable flat hierarchy. Overlap should be explained by cuts and silhouette changes, not shading.
- Judge at thumbnail size. The action and main subject should remain legible before any internal cut is noticed.

## Avoid

- No dense filigree, lace mesh, hairline webs, tiny repeated holes, or wallpaper ornament.
- No broken dashes, interrupted contour fragments, dotted shading, engraving hatches, stippling, or scratchy dry-brush marks.
- No narrow bridges that appear impossible to cut or too fragile to remain attached.
- No repeated Greek-key, snake, worm, insect, scale, cellular, or maze-like filler patterns.
- No vector-perfect mirroring, uniformly spaced decorative perforations, generic floral filler, or identical repeated motifs.
- No drawn outlines inside silhouettes, fake ink strokes, embossed edges, paper-layer shadows, or 3D craft mockup effects.
- Do not outline every source detail. If a detail does not strengthen silhouette or structure, remove it.

The result should read immediately as an expressive hand-cut folk image from a normal viewing distance: bold but not crude, detailed only where the cuts explain identity, action, connection, or rhythm.
