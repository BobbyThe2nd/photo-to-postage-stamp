# Editorial layered paper-cut

Use `SCHEME_EDITORIAL_LAYERED_PAPERCUT` for high-end editorial postcards and stamp artwork built from minimal handmade cut paper, folded card, and restrained paper sculpture.

## Translation logic

- Extract the smallest set of recognizable anchors: primary subject, outer contour, pose or relationship, one or two structural overlaps, and a few environmental cues.
- Choose one core subject as the visual anchor. Reinterpret its proportions and structure through cutting, folding, layering, masking, and negative space rather than mechanically copying detail.
- Convert selected environmental relationships into broad foreground, midground, and background paper planes. Establish hierarchy through scale differences, axes, positive and negative shapes, overlap, and large quiet areas.
- Keep the subject immediately recognizable at thumbnail size. Merge clutter into larger masses and remove details that do not explain identity, depth, material, movement, or narrative relationship.
- For naturally dense subjects such as flowers, feathers, hair, foliage, or fringe, use a limited number of broad curled or folded strips with deliberate gaps. Do not reproduce every strand.

## Paper construction

- Use refined matte cardstock with subtle visible fibers, clean handmade cut edges, slight folds, curled strips, believable layer thickness, and soft localized contact shadows.
- Use natural diffuse light. Shadows should explain overlap and paper thickness, not create dramatic depth.
- Allow separate paper pieces and dimensional layers, but keep the construction economical. One visual anchor, two or three spatial layers, and a few purposeful accents are usually sufficient.
- Avoid plastic 3D, foam board, cheap craft paper, glossy surfaces, excessive bevels, deep drop shadows, scrapbook clutter, and dense decorative filigree.

## Color

- Extract a limited, harmonious palette from the source's most identifying colors.
- Start with warm ivory or another quiet light paper ground. Add one primary color family, one deep structural tone, one pale supporting layer, and at most one restrained accent.
- Prefer solid paper colors with subtle fiber variation. Avoid neon color, rainbow palettes, complex gradients, and photographic surface texture.

## Editorial typography

- Derive a concise primary title from the subject identity, place, action, material, mood, or symbolic meaning. Do not use a year unless the user explicitly supplies and requests it.
- Use a modern minimalist typeface by default. Keep the primary title modest, roughly 4–7% of postcard height, so it supports rather than dominates the paper sculpture.
- Add two to four very small auxiliary groups when useful. Choose meaningful object names, supplied location information, section markers, sequence numbers, direction words, material terms, category labels, or one very short poetic note. Never invent factual metadata.
- Keep auxiliary type thin, precise, widely tracked, and subordinate. Align it to paper edges, subject contours, geometric axes, or negative-space boundaries.
- Use horizontal or vertical placement, edge alignment, restrained rotation, masking, or paper-layer embedding to create a reading path. Limit direction changes and avoid scattered labels.
- The primary title may become a simple cut- or folded-paper letterform; auxiliary text should remain crisp and editorial.

## Relationship to other paper styles

- `SCHEME_CHIN_PAPERCUT` remains a flatter folk narrative based on one connected sheet and readable negative cuts.
- `SCHEME_EDITORIAL_LAYERED_PAPERCUT` allows separate components, folded planes, curled strips, multiple paper depths, and modern micro-typography.
- Both may share matte cardstock, visible fibers, handmade cut edges, and restrained physical paper cues. Do not erase their structural distinction.
