# Naive watercolor bestiary

Use this scheme for animals, pets, birds, insects, and playful natural-history subjects when an expressive handmade portrait matters more than anatomical polish.

## Visual grammar

- Build the subject from 2–6 broad transparent watercolor shapes. Let washes overlap, pool at some edges, feather into damp paper, and vary naturally in density.
- Simplify anatomy into a strong silhouette with intentionally awkward or exaggerated proportions: oversized body, long beak, blunt paws, broad horns, round eyes, or an improbable stance.
- Reserve clean paper white as an active shape, especially around eyes, between limbs, and around the silhouette. Keep the background mostly empty.
- Add only a few irregular black or very dark marks after the washes: facial features, whiskers, claws, stripes, feather divisions, or a loose contour. Allow line weight and alignment to wobble.
- Use 3–5 colors: a dominant earthy or cool family, one supporting neutral, black, paper white, and at most one vivid accent such as vermilion, magenta, citron, or turquoise.
- Preserve handmade evidence such as translucent overlaps, backruns, dry edges, minor registration gaps, and uneven brush pressure. Keep the paper fresh and unaged.

## Bold ink zodiac character lock

Use this locked variant for Chinese zodiac sets and other animal series when the user asks for a memorable, artist-authored character system rather than generic cute watercolor.

### Shape language

- Construct each animal from a memorable silhouette before adding features. Favor blunt, bulky, elongated, curled, top-heavy, or otherwise deliberately awkward masses over polite anatomical accuracy.
- Give every animal one distinct structural gesture: an improbable stance, oversized body, sweeping tail, broad horns, stretched neck, tucked limbs, or compressed crouch. The set should feel related without repeating one body template.
- Keep the animals good-natured and slightly clumsy. They may look alert, skeptical, sleepy, stubborn, or quietly mischievous, but never babyish, vacant, threatening, or conventionally mascot-like.

### Mark grammar

- Build the outer contour primarily from broad watercolor or ink masses, broken dry-brush edges, and pooled pigment. Do not enclose the animal with a continuous thin outline.
- Paint facial structure with the same brush language as the body. Avoid combining polished line-art faces with loose watercolor bodies.
- Use paper-white gaps as structural marks around eyes, muzzle, limbs, and between overlapping forms. Allow irregular registration and asymmetric edges.
- Keep dark marks sparse but decisive: one substantial eyebrow stroke, eyelid, stripe group, nostril, claw, or tail accent is preferable to many descriptive lines.

### Expression system

- Treat the gaze as a character decision, not a universal icon. Vary eye size, openness, direction, and placement across the set.
- Default expression strength is moderate: bold enough to read at stamp size, softened by uneven brush pressure and asymmetry. Avoid identical circular eyes, googly eyes, perfect symmetry, needle-like pupils, aggressive brows, and exaggerated grimaces.
- A slightly sharp gaze is allowed when it belongs to the animal's gesture, but keep it restrained and non-threatening. Preserve an undertone of honesty, awkwardness, or quiet humor.
- For rooster, tiger, and dragon in a zodiac issue, prefer three controlled exceptions: rooster with a small raised side-looking ink eye; crouching tiger with a narrow oblique downward gaze; dragon with a tiny elongated almond or side eye made from one or two irregular brush marks. These eyes may be sharper than the rest of the set, but must not become angry or theatrical.

### Series consistency

- Lock the whole series to fresh warm paper, 3–5 restrained colors, broad transparent washes, concentrated dark ink accents, and generous negative space.
- Preserve different silhouettes and expressions while keeping a shared pigment density, edge roughness, paper-white logic, and level of anatomical simplification.
- When revising a selected sheet, treat approved animal poses, proportions, palette, and facial expressions as binding references. Change only the named animals or features unless the user explicitly requests a broader redesign.

## Composition

- Prefer one large animal occupying 60–80% of the stamp field, cropped only inside the printed artwork while the stamp and perforations remain fully visible.
- Use frontal, strict profile, curled, or stiff standing poses. Favor direct eye contact or a single expressive gesture.
- For several animals, arrange them as a sparse lineup, repeated study, or one dominant subject with a small companion. Do not create a realistic habitat scene.
- On comparison boards or souvenir-sheet margins, handwritten observation notes, dates, or specimen-like numbers may appear only when supplied by the user or clearly labeled as decorative. Keep them outside the stamp image and never invent factual metadata.

## Guardrails

- Do not clean the image into polished vector art, animation concept art, a children's-book scene, photorealistic wildlife art, or traditional misty ink landscape painting.
- Do not use smooth airbrushing, glossy gradients, perfect bilateral symmetry, uniform outlines, dense scenery, sepia aging, foxing, random grunge, or decorative pseudo-writing.
- Do not copy a reference animal, page layout, signature, watermark, or handwriting literally. Derive only the broad medium, mark-making, proportion, palette, and negative-space principles.
- Keep required postal lettering typeset or carefully hand-lettered, exact, legible, and separate from expressive sketchbook marks.
- Do not drift toward generic natural-history watercolor, polished children's-book illustration, uniform mascot design, or a set of animals differentiated only by species markings.
