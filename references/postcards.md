# Postcard design and presentation

Use this workflow when creating a postcard from a stamp, stamp set, photograph, illustration, or described theme.

## Default deliverables

Unless the user requests another format, create:

1. A landscape postcard front at the common 148:100 proportion.
2. A functional back at the identical pixel dimensions and identical 148:100 proportion.
3. One portrait social poster showing both complete sides over a related design-process sheet.
4. One standalone comparison showcase image for the front only:
   - aspect ratio must be portrait `3:4`.
   - reference target size is `100 × 150 mm / 4 × 6 in`.
   - warm white / ivory paper background.
   - place the original reference photo at the top on the page, with natural sticky-tape texture cues.
   - place the generated postcard front in the bottom half.
   - if both the source photo and postcard front are portrait, stack them so the source is behind and the postcard front appears over it.
   - keep both images fully visible with clear separation or overlap relationship.

When the user explicitly asks for before/after, original/artwork, source/translation, or process comparison, also create one source-versus-translation diptych. This is an editorial presentation artifact, not a replacement for the functional back.

Keep the front and back as separate concepts but one physical object. In the social poster, both cards must have exactly the same long-edge length, short-edge length, visible area, and perspective scale. Small rotations and offsets are allowed. Never enlarge the front to manufacture hierarchy.

## Derive rather than repeat

Treat an uploaded stamp as source material, not a sticker or background tile. Extract:

- the subject's identifying anatomy, pose, expression, and relationship;
- palette and contrast;
- line, cut, brush, or print behavior;
- recurring symbols and ornaments;
- implied motion, narrative, and emotional temperature.

Build one new postcard idea from those elements. Do not paste, enlarge, frame, or repeat the complete stamp unless the user explicitly requests a reproduction.

## Front

- Preserve the source subject's key identifying features while allowing purposeful simplification and transformation.
- Establish one clear visual idea and generous negative space.
- Use smooth, continuous watercolor or ink gestures when the source is painterly. Avoid stippled dots, granular blotches, mottled clusters, scab-like texture, and dense repeated marks unless the selected scheme explicitly requires them.
- Use a clear typography hierarchy: one primary title and, when useful, one much smaller secondary line. Keep exact requested wording legible.

### Blue-pencil field-sketch postcards

When using `SCHEME_BLUE_PENCIL_FIELD_SKETCH`, read [blue-pencil-field-sketch.md](blue-pencil-field-sketch.md). Recompose the subject as one generous observational scene rather than enlarging a finished stamp. Let pale construction arcs, scale studies, and one or two incomplete alternate gestures occupy the quiet areas without competing with the resolved subject. Carry only one small blue-pencil emblem or open contour onto the functional back. On the presentation poster, use the same drawing process as the subordinate background sheet so the complete front and back appear to rest over the designer's actual working page.

### Calm solid-wash doodle postcards

When using `SCHEME_CALM_SOLID_WASH_DOODLE`, read [calm-solid-wash-doodle.md](calm-solid-wash-doodle.md). Keep identity and structure in direct water-based felt-tip lines and calm opaque color planes. Use translucent watercolor only as a limited atmospheric field or soft perimeter transition. Let paper white remain visible and avoid turning surfaces into speckles, blooms, or gray ink diffusion. Set titles and short notes in loose hand-drawn marker lettering rather than polished script or typeset display lettering.

## Source-versus-translation diptych

Use this layout only when the user asks to see how the source became the artwork.

- Preserve the source photo unchanged on the `SOURCE` side. Do not repaint, filter, retouch, or copy annotations, watermarks, QR codes, captions, or interface elements from reference images.
- Show the completed postcard artwork on the `TRANSLATION` side at a closely matched crop, subject scale, and horizon or structural alignment so the transformation is easy to read.
- Default to a left/right split for portrait or square source material. Default to a top/bottom split for landscape source material or when the subject reads better across the full width.
- Give both halves equal or near-equal visual weight: prefer 50:50, or at most 46:54 when the artwork needs extra paper breathing room. Use a quiet gutter rather than a decorative divider.
- Preserve the actual postcard at the 148:100 proportion inside the translation field. Do not stretch the card to fill the comparison page.
- Use one small primary title, the two labels, and optionally one meaningful thought fragment. Keep labels outside both images and use no pseudo-language.
- Retain paper texture and negative space around the translation, but do not make the comparison page look like a commercial mockup.

The diptych may be composed at a social 4:5 or 3:4 ratio. It supplements the normal front, back, and two-sided presentation poster; it never replaces them.

## Back

- Keep it genuinely usable: include a divider, stamp box, and three or four address lines.
- Reserve most of the surface for writing and addressing.
- Carry the front's visual language with one small emblem, one edge motif, or one restrained continuous line. Do not reproduce the entire front artwork.
- Omit invented postage, addresses, dates, and narrative copy unless supplied by the user.

## Social presentation poster

Make the cards the unmistakable primary layer. Default each card to roughly 65–72% of the poster width when space permits. Background material may be obscured; it is supporting evidence, not a competing illustration.

Prefer an annotated designer's working sheet over a commercial lifestyle mockup. Use warm drafting paper and source-derived process material such as:

- gesture drawing → refined contour → final color or ink progression;
- anatomy, silhouette, ornament, and subject-to-motif decomposition;
- alternate compositions, including visibly rejected options;
- palette, opacity, wash, brush-pressure, edge, or print-registration tests;
- crop marks, measurement guides, arrows, baselines, and registration crosses;
- exploratory lines that continue beneath the finished cards.

Keep process drawings pale graphite, faded source color, or light wash. Preserve generous quiet areas. Do not use tables, cloth, coffee, glass, flowers, stationery, tape, or other styled flat-lay props unless the user explicitly requests them.

## Typography as process graphics

Add a restrained text layer to the exposed design sheet when it improves the composition:

- one source-derived background title;
- two to four short thought fragments describing motion, form, or concept;
- small technical labels for color, direction, scale, or study number;
- optionally one compact English index line.

Use only meaningful, legible wording. Mix horizontal, vertical, curved, and scattered placement so text can act as line, counterweight, and rhythm. Do not align everything into a report, cover the finished cards, invent factual metadata, or generate paragraphs of pseudo-language.

## Prompt invariants

Repeat these requirements in every generation and edit prompt:

- Front and back are one card and must remain exactly the same size and aspect ratio.
- Keep both cards complete and uncropped.
- Preserve approved card artwork and exact lettering during background or layout revisions.
- Finished cards dominate; process material is subordinate and may be covered.
- Avoid commercial product-mockup lighting and lifestyle props.
- No QR code or watermark.

If either side is rendered at a different size, correct the mismatch before presenting the result.
