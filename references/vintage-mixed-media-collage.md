# Vintage mixed-media collage

Use `SCHEME_VINTAGE_MIXED_MEDIA_COLLAGE` when the result should feel physically assembled, joyfully direct, visibly handmade, and charged like a rough theatrical or festival poster rather than digitally polished or reduced to modernist flat geometry.

## Visual language

- Build the image from a small number of bold hand-cut paper masses, preserving the source subject's recognizable silhouette, pose, and essential structure.
- Redraw the entire subject from scratch. Use the source only to understand silhouette, placement, gesture, perspective, and essential color relationships.
- Never retain, trace over, filter, collage in, or visibly reproduce photographic pixels, photographic rock or foliage texture, camera lighting, lens detail, image noise, or realistic surface rendering.
- Use hot color collisions such as cobalt with vermilion, coral with turquoise, acid yellow with deep blue, or pink with forest green. Keep one quiet paper field so the contrast remains legible rather than noisy.
- Combine several genuinely distinct marks: wax-crayon drag, chalk-pastel haze, colored-pencil hatch, dry-brush scrape, coarse screen-print or photocopy grain, torn or scissored paper edges, and sparse ink accents.
- Accept imperfect registration, rubbed pigment, uneven opacity, exposed paper, accidental overlaps, blunt corrections, and asymmetry. These should look incidental and localized, not applied as a uniform distress filter.
- Keep rough printing visibly present across the composition through uneven ink density, missed coverage, rubbed transitions, dry edges, and slight registration shift. It should unify the mixed media without becoming one repeated digital grain layer.
- Use sparse irregular white or paper-colored missed-ink flecks as a preferred vintage-print artifact. Vary fleck size, shape, spacing, orientation, density, and clustering strongly; include isolated pinholes, short broken scratches, and a few loose constellations with broad quiet gaps between them.
- Use loose, awkward, human handwriting for short titles or notes. Keep required postal wording deterministic, readable, and separate from expressive annotation.
- Favor one large subject or a small conversational group, a few energetic symbols, and generous unworked space. Do not decorate every available surface.

## Simplification and density

- Identify no more than 3–5 essential recognition anchors before drawing. Examples include the outer mountain silhouette, one road perspective, one hero vehicle, one cloud group, or one dominant animal gesture.
- Merge minor terrain, vegetation, architecture, crowds, or repeated objects into one or two broad color masses. Remove details that do not change recognition.
- Keep roughly 50–70% of the composition as exposed paper, flat unmarked color, or broad low-detail shapes.
- Limit active texture to a few localized passages occupying no more than roughly one third of the image. Never texture every mountain, plant, road, sky, and object simultaneously.
- Use at most two mark families in any one area, such as crayon plus dry brush. Leave adjacent areas quiet.
- Separate foreground, subject, and background through silhouette and color contrast rather than dense internal marks.
- For landscapes, redraw the land as large cut-paper planes. Suggest strata with a few irregular strokes; do not describe individual rocks, gravel, shrubs, leaves, or photographic mottling.
- For roads or repeated vehicles, preserve the main perspective and one or two identity anchors; simplify distant traffic into sparse block shapes or omit it.

## Structural pattern clusters

Pattern is a secondary but necessary element in this scheme. Without it, the result collapses into flat geometric color and loses the visual richness and dramatic tension of a handmade festival poster.

### Texture is not pattern

- **Texture / material ground:** continuous colored-pencil grain, chalk-pastel haze, wax-crayon drag, rubbed pigment, dry-brush coverage, uneven screen-print ink, and irregular missed-ink noise. Texture changes how a color field feels and transitions; it does not form a repeated symbol.
- **Pattern / discrete motif:** visible hand-drawn dots, short dashes, tiny bars, small oval rings, circles, scallops, wave lines, hooks, and occasional individual spirals. Pattern creates rhythm, detail, direction, emphasis, and local identity on top of the material texture.
- Use both layers. A successful area may contain a colored-pencil or pastel texture ground plus one localized family of discrete pattern marks.

- Add a small number of concentrated, fine-scale mark clusters after the main collage shapes are established.
- Every cluster must explain something visible: foreground versus background, overlap, a fold or structural turn, light-to-shadow change, material identity, movement, anatomy, terrain direction, or a focal detail.
- Place clusters at transitions, joints, folds, edges, shadow boundaries, directional flows, or recognition-critical zones. Do not place them in arbitrary empty centers merely to fill space.
- Build each cluster from many small marks with varied length, pressure, color, spacing, and direction. Choose dots, short lines, tiny bars, oval rings, circles, scallops, waves, hooks, or occasional spirals according to the form. Let density peak where the structural change is strongest and taper gradually into the surrounding color.
- Match the mark family to the depicted form: short directional hatches for a turning plane, curved marks for rounded anatomy, horizontal broken ticks for strata, aligned drags for movement or perspective, and tonal overlaps for light-to-shadow transition.
- Keep clusters visually subordinate to silhouette and major color masses, but do not omit them entirely. A typical image should contain several purposeful clusters of different character rather than one generic texture repeated everywhere.
- Avoid oversized parallel bars or a few broad decorative lines pretending to be detail. The cluster should reward closer viewing through numerous small handmade decisions.
- Balance active and quiet zones. Quiet color makes each structural cluster legible; the goal is dramatic contrast and material richness, not minimalist emptiness or uniform busyness.
- Small circles, oval rings, waves, and spirals are allowed when individually drawn, irregular, interrupted, localized, and structurally motivated. Do not ban a motif merely because it is recognizable.

## Print noise, pattern, and artificial repetition

Irregular white print noise and recognizable handmade pattern are both allowed. Dense mechanical repetition is not.

- Treat white flecks as physical ink dropout: non-periodic, interrupted, sparse, differently sized, and unevenly clustered.
- Let missed ink be slightly more visible in selected dark printed areas and near dry roller passages, then disappear into clean color.
- Keep most flecks tiny, with occasional short scratches or larger missed patches. Never make every mark the same size or distribute them evenly.
- White noise must remain accidental ink dropout and must not organize itself into a pattern.
- Pattern marks may form small local groups, but must vary visibly in size, spacing, orientation, pressure, completeness, and color. They must stop before becoming a field, band, border, grid, chain, or continuous network.
- Allow a few distinct circles, oval rings, wave fragments, hooks, scallops, or spirals. Do not tile them across a whole surface or connect them into a maze-like route.
- Remove snake-skin fields, fish-scale fields, Greek-key bands, worm or insect trails, cellular membranes, dense lace, wallpaper, pseudo-writing, or any motif repeated with machine-like consistency.

## Authorship and reference boundary

- Treat supplied examples as evidence of materials, contrast, spacing, and tolerance for imperfection, not as compositions to copy.
- Create original collage shapes, gestures, lettering, symbols, and color relationships. Do not reproduce reference captions, signatures, watermarks, or distinctive arrangements.
- Preserve natural and architectural geometry unless the user explicitly requests magical transformation. Style may change; mountains remain mountains, water remains water, sky remains sky, and built structures retain their identity.

## Anti-pattern rules

- Never manufacture dense repeated Greek-key bands, snake-skin fields, fish-scale fields, worm tracks, insect trails, cellular tessellations, pseudo-writing, or maze-like filler. Individual hand-drawn waves, rings, hooks, scallops, and spirals remain allowed as localized pattern vocabulary.
- Never repeat one decorative squiggle at uniform spacing across the subject. Repetition must come from observed anatomy or source structure, not generated pattern filler.
- Avoid all-over halftone, all-over noise, uniform speckling, synthetic fabric patterns, wallpaper effects, and identical distress on every color plane.
- Avoid photo-plus-collage results, painterly photo filters, edge-traced photographs, realistic rock noise, realistic foliage noise, and texture mapped onto every source detail.
- If an area needs material variation, use one broad rubbed passage, several irregular hand marks, a torn-paper boundary, or leave the paper exposed.
- Keep high-energy color separate from high-density texture. When color is loud, reduce mark count; when marks are dense, narrow the palette.
